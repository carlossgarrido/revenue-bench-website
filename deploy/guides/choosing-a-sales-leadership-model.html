CRO, VP of Sales, or fractional: choosing the sales leadership model

By Carlos Garrido, Co-Founder, Revenue BenchPublished August 3, 2026

The sales-leadership model should follow the revenue motion the company runs today. A founder-led company forming its first playbook needs different ownership from a company coordinating sales, marketing, and customer expansion. The matrix below uses observable operating conditions to choose among a CRO, a full-time VP of Sales, a fractional leader, and no full-time leader yet.

Key takeaways

  • The leadership model follows the company's stage, and that stage is visible in who sells, how the motion repeats, and which functions must work together.
  • Match the model to the revenue motion the company runs today.
  • Hiring a CRO before CRO scope exists is the most expensive version of the mistake.
  • Whichever model the company chooses, the leader owns recruiting and builds the future talent bench.

The answer first

The correct model follows the stage of the revenue motion. Look at who closes, whether the sales process repeats without founder intervention, how much forecast discipline matters, and whether responsibility spans sales alone or the broader revenue engine. Titles can obscure those facts, so the useful question is what the role controls.

A Chief Revenue Officer owns revenue across sales, marketing alignment, and customer expansion. The defining feature is that cross-functional scope. The CRO sets the operating system for the whole revenue engine, resolves conflicts between functions, allocates attention across acquisition and expansion, and carries the combined revenue outcome. A CRO title over a sales-only mandate leaves the company with a VP of Sales role and CRO-level compensation.

A VP of Sales owns the sales team. The role includes recruiting, operating cadence, pipeline discipline, forecast quality, coaching, performance management, and execution against the sales plan. A strong VP can partner closely with marketing and customer leadership without owning those functions. This model fits when the sales motion repeats, the team is growing, and the company needs one full-time operator accountable for the sales number.

A fractional sales leader is a part-time experienced operator who installs structure without a full-time cost. The work can include cadence, pipeline definitions, manager routines, an initial hiring process, and clearer accountability. The model fits a transitional stage where the company needs operating discipline but lacks enough enduring leadership scope for a full-time executive. Its value comes from a defined installation mandate and a visible handoff point.

Founder-led with structure means no leadership hire yet. The founder remains the primary seller while documenting the sales process, defining pipeline stages, reviewing deals on a regular cadence, and preparing a playbook that another seller can use. This is a legitimate operating choice. Hiring senior leadership before there is a team or repeatable motion gives the new executive little to lead and asks the role to discover a business model under an executive title. The sequencing of those first sellers, and what must exist before each offer, is covered in the founder's first sales hires.

Many companies take a fifth path by promoting the strongest seller into management. That is a decision about one person's fit for management work, and it deserves its own evidence. The separate analysis of whether to promote your best rep covers the evidence required before making that move.

The failure patterns

The wrong leadership model usually fails through a mismatch between title, mandate, and company stage. The person may be capable while the role remains impossible to perform as designed.

The CRO hired too early

The company wants executive weight and recruits a CRO while the available job controls only sales. The title implies authority across marketing alignment, acquisition, sales, and expansion, but the operating mandate covers a single function. Compensation and expectations reflect broader responsibility while decision rights do not, and under a sponsor that mismatch is written into the leader's package before anyone notices it.

The new executive either works as an over-titled VP of Sales or tries to influence functions held by other leaders. Friction follows because the company never decided which outcomes, budgets, and teams belong to the role. The mis-scoped hire usually does not last, and the company begins another search without fixing the underlying design.

The VP hired too late

A founder can remain an effective seller after adding reps, but the model breaks when every forecast judgment, deal exception, coaching conversation, and hiring decision waits on the founder. Reps learn to escalate rather than operate, managers lack authority, and the forecast becomes a collection of opinions reconciled by one person.

The signal is a motion that repeats while every decision still routes through the founder. Once the motion works without the founder in every deal, a full-time VP can own the system around it. Waiting beyond that point turns the founder's selling strength into an operating constraint, and each open month carries a price you can compute with the vacancy ledger.

The fractional engagement treated as permanent

A fractional leader can install structure, cadence, pipeline definitions, review routines, and an initial recruiting process. That mandate works when the company needs experienced design and temporary operating discipline. It weakens when the engagement becomes the standing answer to a role that now requires full-time ownership.

Long-horizon recruiting and accountability for the number need a person whose whole professional weight sits on this one company. The handoff trigger should be defined when the engagement begins and reviewed as the motion develops. A fractional leader is usually engaged through a firm or as a contractor rather than as an employee. How the company directs and controls that work bears on worker classification, and the arrangement is worth running past employment counsel before it is set.

The best rep promoted by default

Promoting the strongest seller can fill an organizational gap while creating another one. Selling performance does not establish recruiting ability, coaching discipline, forecast judgment, or willingness to hold peers accountable. If the company needs a manager, evaluate the candidate against management work. If it needs a VP or CRO, define that scope before considering an internal title change.

Whichever model you choose, the leader recruits

One responsibility stays constant across the models: the leader builds the talent supply required by the plan. In Carlos Garrido's sales-leadership curriculum, the operating rule is that recruitment is the leader's prospecting. A sales leader who is not building a bench of future hires is not doing the leadership job, whatever the title.

That work starts before a vacancy opens. The leader maintains relationships with prospective hires, defines what strong performance means in the company's motion, keeps role scorecards current, and understands which capability the team will need next. A fractional leader can establish that discipline. A VP owns it for the sales organization. A CRO makes sure talent planning supports the broader revenue design.

Recruiting ownership also prevents a common handoff failure. A recruiter can find and assess candidates, but the leader must define the job, test operating judgment, explain the mandate, and decide whether the person can carry it. Delegating the whole decision leaves the company selecting against a title instead of the work.

The model is one decision. Selecting a person against that model's scope is a separate one, and it needs its own evidence. In Objective Management Group's dataset of 44,493 sales managers, only 9% are strong in all three of the qualities that develop elite sellers, and the managers who hold all three produce 80% more elite salespeople than the ones who do not. Those are OMG's own figures, and they show why leadership selection needs evidence beyond prior title and team size.

Frequently asked

Questions on choosing the model, answered

What is the difference between a CRO and a VP of Sales?

A CRO owns the whole revenue engine across sales, marketing alignment, and customer expansion. A VP of Sales owns the sales team, including recruiting, cadence, pipeline discipline, forecast, and coaching. The distinction comes from scope and decision rights rather than seniority. A CRO whose mandate covers only sales is a VP of Sales with a bigger title.

When should a company hire its first VP of Sales?

Hire the first VP when the sales motion repeats without the founder participating in every deal, the team needs sustained management, and forecast quality begins to affect company decisions. Those operating markers matter more than revenue or headcount thresholds. The role should inherit a motion it can manage and improve, along with clear authority over recruiting, coaching, pipeline, and performance.

When does a fractional sales leader make sense?

A fractional leader makes sense when a company needs experienced help installing sales structure and cadence but does not have enough enduring scope or cost capacity for a full-time leader. The engagement should have a defined mandate, such as pipeline standards, manager routines, or recruiting discipline, plus a handoff trigger. It should not become permanent cover for a full-time accountability gap.

When should a company not hire a sales leader at all?

When the founder still closes every deal and no repeatable process exists for a leader to run. At that stage the highest-value work is documenting the sales process, defining pipeline stages, and building a playbook another seller can use. Hiring senior leadership before there is a team or a repeatable motion gives the executive nothing to lead. A bounded fractional engagement can install structure while the company grows into the next stage.

What happens when a company hires a CRO too early?

The company creates a title-inflation problem. The CRO title suggests ownership across the revenue engine, while the available authority may cover only sales. The executive is then judged against outcomes controlled by other leaders or operates as an over-titled VP. Friction grows around decisions and accountability, and the hire often ends before the company corrects the role design.

When should a VP of Sales role become a CRO role?

The role should become a CRO role when revenue responsibility genuinely spans sales, marketing alignment, and customer expansion, and the company needs one executive to govern the interlocks among them. The change follows an expansion of mandate, decision rights, and accountability. A title change without that broader scope leaves the company with the same VP of Sales role under a different label.
