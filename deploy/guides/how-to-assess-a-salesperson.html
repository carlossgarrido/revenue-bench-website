How to assess sales candidates before hiring

By Carlos Garrido, Co-Founder, Revenue Bench11 min read

Assess a sales candidate on evidence the resume cannot supply. A resume records results, and a sales result depends on the territory, the product, the accounts inherited, and the comp plan as much as on the person. Five steps separate the two: a written role profile, a sales-specific assessment, a structured interview that tests every resume claim, a work sample with live coaching, and scored references.

Key takeaways

  • Assess one candidate in five steps, in order: role profile, sales-specific assessment, structured interview, work sample, then scoring and references.
  • Every resume claim is a result. Separate it from the territory, product, accounts, and comp plan that also produced it before it counts as evidence about the person.
  • Four traits predict sales performance: will to sell, coachability, qualification discipline, and process fit.
  • The assessment runs before interview time is spent, so the interview impression does not grade it.

How to assess a sales candidate, step by step

To assess a sales candidate before hiring, run five steps in order and let each one decide a single question. Revenue Bench's sequence is to write the role profile, run a sales-specific assessment before interviews, use a structured interview to test the resume's claims and the candidate's buyer level, watch a work sample with a scripted coaching prompt, then score the evidence against a written rubric and confirm it with references the candidate has agreed to.

  1. Write the role profile before the search opens. It decides what evidence will count. Write down the sales motion, including cycle length, deal size, and buyer level, plus the four traits below. Define weak, solid, and strong evidence for each so every later step uses the same standard.
  2. Run the sales-specific assessment first. It decides whether the person can sell in this role's environment through will to sell, Sales DNA, and fit with the motion. Run it before interviews so the interview impression does not grade the result; see why the order matters.
  3. Use a structured interview to test the resume. It decides whether the resume's results belong to the person and at what buyer level the person sells. Walk the deal history one deal at a time using the resume-claim check below, then ask the two buyer-level questions.
  4. Watch a work sample with a scripted coaching prompt. It decides qualification discipline and coachability through observation. Run a mock discovery call containing a fact that should cause a disciplined seller to disqualify the opportunity. Give every candidate the same coaching prompt, then score whether the second attempt changes.
  5. Score the evidence, then check references. It decides whether the evidence holds. Have each interviewer score against the written rubric before the group discusses the candidate. Then ask references the candidate has agreed to confirm cycle length, deal size, buyer level, and rank against peers.

The five-step sequence is Revenue Bench's own selection guidance. In a Revenue Bench search, Revenue Bench and the hiring manager write and approve the success profile, and Revenue Bench runs the assessment and operator screen. The interview, work sample, rubric, and references are the employer's.

How to evaluate a sales candidate beyond experience

A sales resume reports results, and every sales result depends on conditions besides the seller: the territory, the product, the accounts inherited, the comp plan, and the team around the deal. To evaluate a candidate beyond experience, take each claim on the resume, list what else could have produced it, and ask the question that separates the person's contribution from those conditions.

Resume claim What else can produce it The question that separates the person Where to verify it
Quota attainment A quota set low, a strong market year, a large inherited book of accounts, one outsized deal, or a quota cut partway through the year. "What was the quota, how was it set, and how did the rest of the team do against theirs?" Then: "How much of it came from accounts you opened?" The deal-by-deal walk in the interview, a former manager asked where the candidate ranked against peers, and any rankings or awards the candidate volunteers. Many employers bar managers from confirming quota figures.
Top-performer ranking or sales award A small team, a ranking on a single metric, one strong year, or a territory reassignment. "How many sellers were ranked, on what measure, and where did you finish the year before and the year after?" A former manager, asked where the candidate ranked against peers.
Well-known customer logos Accounts inherited from a predecessor, deals led by a senior seller or an executive sponsor, or renewals of existing contracts. "Which of these accounts did you open, and which part of each sale did you run yourself?" The deal walk in the structured interview, then a reference who can confirm the candidate's role in specific deals.
Revenue closed or book size Renewals and expansion counted with new business, team selling credited to one name, or price increases. "How much of that was net-new, and what were the average deal size and sales cycle?" The structured interview, then references asked to confirm cycle length and deal size.
Years of experience and title A title inflated by company size, the same first year repeated, or a sales motion unlike yours. "Who did you sell to, who made the decision, and how long did a typical deal take?" The buyer-level screen below, confirmed in reference checks.

The resume-claim check is Revenue Bench's own selection guidance. A claim that survives it becomes evidence about the person. When an employer's policy stops a reference from confirming a claim, record the claim as unverified and give it no weight in either direction. A candidate who cannot state the quota, the deal size, or which accounts they opened has answered the track-record question, and the account executive interview scorecard scores that answer.

Some employers limit what a reference can confirm, and a candidate may know the quota without knowing how it was set. Record those gaps as unverified. The assessment and work sample supply the evidence a resume cannot hold.

The four traits that predict performance

Four traits predict sales performance, and a resume shows none of them directly. Measure each one in your role.

  • Will to sell. The drive to pursue revenue, the comfort with rejection, and the desire to win. Without it, skill and training go nowhere because the seller will avoid the hard parts of the job when no one is watching.
  • Coachability. The willingness to accept feedback and change behavior. Watch what the candidate does with coaching during an exercise, since an agreeable answer about feedback costs nothing to give.
  • Qualification discipline. The habit of qualifying hard and walking away from deals that will not close. Strong sellers protect their time and report the pipeline based on evidence.
  • Process fit. The ability to execute inside your specific sales motion. A transactional closer can stall in a long enterprise sale, while an enterprise seller may move too slowly for a high-velocity SMB motion. Match the sales history to the role.

These traits rarely surface on paper or emerge on their own in a friendly conversation. That is the core problem with how most companies hire sellers.

The trait-evidence matrix

For each of the four traits, the matrix sets out the evidence that counts, the false positive interviews keep accepting, and the screen that measures it.

Trait The question it settles Evidence that counts The false positive interviews accept Where the evidence comes from
Will to sell Will this person do the hard parts when no one is watching? Assessment-measured drive across Desire, Commitment, Motivation, Responsibility, and Outlook, confirmed by the candidate's behavior when the prospect pushes back. Interview enthusiasm, energy, and confidence, which candidates can perform on demand. The sales-specific assessment, confirmed in the work sample.
Coachability Will this person take feedback and change? Response to live coaching inside the work sample. Deliver the same pre-scripted coaching prompt to every candidate for the role, then score whether the next attempt changes. Agreeable answers about loving feedback, which cost nothing to say. The work sample, plus references asked how the person responded to coaching.
Qualification discipline Will the pipeline be real? How the candidate handles a scenario designed to be walked away from, including whether they ask disqualifying questions in the mock discovery call. Stories about big wins, which reveal nothing about deals the candidate should have dropped. The mock discovery call, scored on the rubric.
Process fit Can this person execute in our specific motion? Selling history with a comparable cycle length, deal size, and buyer level, verified in references, plus the buyer-level answer below. A strong track record in a different motion, which can make a mismatched hire look credible. The structured interview and reference checks, measured against the written role profile.

The matrix is Revenue Bench's summary of its own selection guidance. It is not a survey finding.

The buyer-level screen

Before you hire, establish who the candidate has sold to, C-level, VP, director, or manager, and compare that level with who buys from you. A rep who has only sold to procurement managers will struggle in a sale your buyers expect to run at CEO level. The reverse mismatch wastes a senior seller on a transactional motion.

Ask two direct questions in the structured interview: who did you sell to in your last role, and who decided. Verify both answers through the examples the candidate gives and through reference checks.

Carlos Garrido teaches this screen in his Building a Scalable Sales Team masterclass: establish the level a candidate sells at before you hire them, because a mismatch with your buyer surfaces after the start date, when it is most expensive.

Where the assessment fits

The interview is the least reliable screen for selling ability, because selling and interviewing draw on the same skills: rapport, storytelling, reading the other person, and handling objections. A sales-specific assessment measures selling ability and sales DNA without the interview impression dominating the result, which is why it runs first. The full routing, including what an interview decides that an assessment cannot, is in which screen answers which hiring question.

At Revenue Bench, candidates for selling and sales-leadership roles are assessed with Objective Management Group, and every candidate is screened by a sales operator against the written success profile. Candidate assessment runs through Swanston Growth Advisors, a Certified Partner of Objective Management Group. OMG publishes predictive validity in the 95 percent range for its sales-specific instrument, its own accuracy metric. The hiring outcome is a separate figure: in OMG's 2024 validation survey, 72% of recommended hires reached the top half of their sales force within 12 months.

Hire for drive and train the skill. In OMG's study of 5,331 salespeople measured before and after training by a certified OMG partner, Closing rose 55% and Comfort Discussing Money 66%, while Will to Sell moved between 0% and 6%. The Will to Sell guide sets out the five markers of Will to Sell.

Carlos Garrido poses a direct question in his Building a Scalable Sales Team masterclass: "Did you hire them like that, or did you make them like that?" His point is that a team's performance ceiling is set at hiring time more often than leaders admit. Assess before the offer so leaders do not depend on coaching to repair a selection mistake.

Behavioral-style instruments such as DISC describe general working style and were not designed to predict on-the-job sales performance. Why a personality test cannot decide selling ability covers the limit in full.

The same instrument can evaluate a team already in place, helping a buyer assess a target's sales organization during sales team due diligence for private equity buyers.

Use a real work sample

A live work sample shows how someone handles a version of the job. For sellers, use an exercise that reflects the sales motion and exposes the traits in the matrix.

  1. A mock discovery call. Give the candidate a realistic buyer scenario and have a team member play the prospect. Watch how they open, qualify, ask questions, and listen. Include information that should lead a disciplined seller to disqualify the opportunity.
  2. A real-deal role-play. Give the candidate a deal from your pipeline, allow preparation, and ask them to move it forward. Strip client names and confidential terms from any pipeline deal used in a role-play, since the candidate is an outside party.
  3. A short written or live plan. For senior roles, ask the candidate to outline how they would approach a territory or advance a stalled opportunity. Score judgment and process. Exclude presentation design from the score.

Prepared interview answers provide limited evidence because candidates can rehearse them. A work sample shows how the person sells, responds to pressure, and applies feedback. Keep each exercise short and time-boxed, and do not put a candidate's plan or deal strategy to commercial use. The exercise exists to produce hiring evidence, and the work product belongs to the candidate.

Score with a rubric, and check references that matter

Even useful evidence gets wasted when every interviewer judges by feel. A written account executive rubric defines the evidence expected for will to sell, coachability, qualification discipline, and process fit. Sales-leader roles get their own scorecard because leadership evidence differs from individual selling evidence. Define weak, solid, and strong performance for each criterion before interviews begin, then compare recorded evidence.

Use the same exercises and the same rubric for every candidate in the role, keep both unchanged for the length of the search, record the evidence behind each score, and have counsel review the loop, since a scored selection process is a selection procedure.

Finish with reference checks run with the candidate's consent. For an individual contributor, ask the former manager whether they would put this person back on their team today and why, how the candidate responded to coaching, and where the candidate ranked against peers. For process fit, verify cycle length, deal size, buyer level, and who made the final decision. Some employers limit what a reference can confirm, so record a policy-limited answer as unavailable and do not treat it as evidence against the candidate. The full consent sequence and the ten questions are in reference check questions for sales candidates.

Frequently asked

How do you assess a salesperson before hiring?

Run five steps in order: write the role profile, run a sales-specific assessment before interviews, use a structured interview to test the resume's claims and the candidate's buyer level, watch a work sample with a scripted coaching prompt, and score the evidence against a written rubric before confirming it with reference checks the candidate has agreed to. Each step decides one question, and an interview alone is the least reliable signal.

How do you evaluate a sales candidate beyond experience?

Treat each resume claim as a result and separate the person from the conditions that produced it. Ask how quota was set and how the rest of the team performed, which accounts on the resume the candidate opened personally, how much revenue was net-new, and who the candidate sold to and who decided. Verify the answers with a former manager, then add the evidence a resume cannot hold: a sales-specific assessment and a work sample.

How do you verify a sales candidate's quota attainment?

Ask what the quota was, how it was set, and how the rest of the team did against theirs, then ask how much of the result came from accounts the candidate opened. Many employers will not let a former manager confirm quota figures, so ask the manager where the candidate ranked against peers and review any rankings or awards the candidate volunteers. Record a policy-limited answer as unavailable and do not count it against the candidate. Several US states and cities restrict asking about prior pay, so keep compensation out of the questions and have counsel review the process.

What is a sales assessment and which one should I use?

A sales assessment is a validated instrument that measures selling ability and sales DNA directly, separate from how the candidate presents. Revenue Bench assesses candidates for selling and sales-leadership roles with Objective Management Group's sales-specific assessment, which OMG built to predict selling behavior and for which it publishes predictive validity in the 95 percent range, its own accuracy metric.

What work sample should I use to test a salesperson?

A mock discovery call is the most revealing, since discovery is where most deals are won or lost. A real-deal role-play using an actual opportunity from your pipeline is a strong second, and for senior roles, a short territory or deal plan tests judgment. A live selling exercise provides evidence that a behavioral answer cannot.

Should I hire for skill or for drive?

Hire for drive, train the skill. OMG data shows tactical skills respond well to training, with Closing rising 55% and Comfort Discussing Money 66% after training, while Will to Sell moves between 0% and 6% across the same training. You can teach someone to close and qualify, but you cannot install the desire to do the hard parts of the job. Screen for sales DNA and drive before the offer, then develop the tactical skills after.
