How to assess a salesperson beyond the resume

By Carlos Garrido, Co-Founder, Revenue Bench9 min read

The interview is the least reliable part of sales hiring. Because selling and interviewing draw on many of the same presentation skills, a polished interview tells you little about closing under pressure. To predict performance, measure four things: will to sell, coachability, qualification discipline, and whether the candidate can execute inside your specific sales motion. Then test for them with an objective assessment, a real work sample, and reference checks that ask the hard questions.

  • Four traits predict sales performance: will to sell, coachability, qualification discipline, and process fit. A resume shows none of them.
  • Because interviewing and selling use similar presentation skills, polish can conceal weak selling discipline.
  • A sales-specific assessment measures sales DNA and selling ability beyond what a conversation can surface.
  • A work sample, a written rubric, and pointed reference checks turn a gut decision into an evidence-based one.

The four traits that predict performance

A resume tells you where someone has been. It does not tell you whether they will sell for you. Quota attainment at a prior company can reflect a strong market, an established product, inherited accounts, or a generous compensation plan as easily as it reflects the person. To predict performance in your role, measure four traits.

  • Will to sell. The drive to pursue revenue, the comfort with rejection, and the desire to win. Without it, skill and training go nowhere because the seller will avoid the hard parts of the job when no one is watching.
  • Coachability. The willingness to accept feedback and change behavior. Watch what the candidate does with coaching during an exercise, since an agreeable answer about feedback costs nothing to give.
  • Qualification discipline. The habit of qualifying hard and walking away from deals that will not close. Strong sellers protect their time and report the pipeline based on evidence.
  • Process fit. The ability to execute inside your specific sales motion. A transactional closer can stall in a long enterprise sale, while an enterprise seller may move too slowly for a high-velocity SMB motion. Match the sales history to the role.

These traits rarely surface on paper or emerge on their own in a friendly conversation. That is the core problem with how most companies hire sellers.

The selling-altitude screen

Before you hire, establish who the candidate has sold to, C-level, VP, director, or manager, and compare that level with who buys from you. A rep who has only sold to procurement managers will struggle in a sale your buyers expect to run at CEO level. The reverse mismatch wastes a senior seller on a transactional motion.

Ask two direct questions in the structured interview: who did you sell to in your last role, and who decided. Verify both answers through the examples the candidate gives and through reference checks.

Carlos Garrido teaches this screen in his Building a Scalable Sales Team masterclass: establish the level a candidate sells at before you hire them, because a mismatch with your buyer surfaces after the start date, when it is most expensive.

Why interviews are unreliable for sales

The interview is the least reliable part of a sales hiring process. Selling and interviewing draw on the same skills: rapport, storytelling, reading the other person, and handling objections. Because candidates can apply those skills directly to the interview, polished presentation can conceal weak qualification, inconsistent follow-through, or poor closing discipline. A methodical closer may also present with less energy while bringing stronger sales habits.

There is another trap. Interviewers reward people who look and sound like sellers they have known. That pattern matching favors polish and confidence, which sales candidates know how to perform. The result is a hiring decision built on familiarity instead of evidence. Add objective signal the interview cannot give you, then use the interview to test role-specific evidence.

The role of an objective assessment

A validated, sales-specific assessment measures selling ability and sales DNA without allowing interview performance to dominate the result. It is built to predict selling behavior, including will to sell, the hidden weaknesses that hold a seller back, and fit with the demands of a specific sales role.

What a sales-specific assessment measures

OMG measures 21 sales-specific competencies across more than 400 data points. The competencies cover the candidate's drive, the beliefs and tendencies that support or limit selling, and the tactical skills required to execute.

  • Will to Sell. The drive behind the role, measured across Desire, Commitment, Motivation, Responsibility, and Outlook. Screen it before the offer because training has little effect on this group.
  • Sales DNA. The strengths and weaknesses that support or interfere with selling: Comfort Discussing Money, Need for Approval, Controls Emotions and Stays in the Moment, Supportive Beliefs, a Supportive Buy Cycle, and Handles Rejection. An interview rarely exposes these factors.
  • Tactical Selling. The skills of the job itself: Hunting, Reaching Decision Makers, Relationship Building, Consultative Selling, Selling Value, Qualifying, Presentation Approach, Closing, Sales Process, and Sales Technology.

OMG's 2022-to-2025 data shows only 12 percent of salespeople are strong on Supportive Beliefs, so 88 percent carry beliefs that cap them. OMG also reports only 11 to 15 percent strong in Consultative Selling and only 16 percent proficient in Sales Technology in 2025. In the other direction, Handles Rejection is strong in 88 to 89 percent of salespeople and Desire in 86 percent, so those common strengths separate candidates less effectively than the competencies an interview fails to reach.

Train the skill, hire the drive

OMG studied 5,331 salespeople measured before and after training by a certified OMG partner. After training, OMG measured Closing up 55 percent, Reaching Decision Makers up 43 percent, Consultative Selling up 28 percent, Selling Value up 24 percent, and Qualifying up 20 percent, with Comfort Discussing Money up 66 percent. Training lifted overall sales percentile by about 30 percent, which OMG translates as the gap between a rep under 70 percent of quota and one over 90 percent.

Will to Sell moves between 0 and 6 percent across the same training. Hire for drive and coachability, then train tactical gaps. Coaching is covered in a separate OMG study, and Will to Sell explains what coaching does and does not reach.

Carlos Garrido poses a direct question in his Building a Scalable Sales Team masterclass: "Did you hire them like that, or did you make them like that?" His point is that a team's performance ceiling is set at hiring time more often than leaders admit. Assess before the offer so leaders do not depend on coaching to repair a selection mistake.

Behavioral-style instruments such as DISC describe general working style. They were built for that purpose and later adapted for hiring, and they are not designed to predict on-the-job sales performance. A sales-specific assessment is built to predict selling behavior.

Use a real work sample

A live work sample shows how someone handles a version of the job. For sellers, use an exercise that reflects the sales motion and exposes the traits in the matrix.

  1. A mock discovery call. Give the candidate a realistic buyer scenario and have a team member play the prospect. Watch how they open, qualify, ask questions, and listen. Include information that should lead a disciplined seller to disqualify the opportunity.
  2. A real-deal role-play. Give the candidate a deal from your pipeline, allow preparation, and ask them to move it forward. Strip client names and confidential terms from any pipeline deal used in a role-play, since the candidate is an outside party.
  3. A short written or live plan. For senior roles, ask the candidate to outline how they would approach a territory or advance a stalled opportunity. Score judgment and process rather than presentation design.

Prepared interview answers provide limited evidence because candidates can rehearse them. A work sample shows how the person sells, responds to pressure, and applies feedback. Keep each exercise short and time-boxed, and do not put a candidate's plan or deal strategy to commercial use. The exercise exists to produce hiring evidence, and the work product belongs to the candidate.

Score with a rubric, and check references that matter

Even useful evidence gets wasted when every interviewer judges by feel. A written account executive rubric defines the evidence expected for will to sell, coachability, qualification discipline, and process fit. Sales-leader roles get their own scorecard because leadership evidence differs from individual selling evidence. Define weak, solid, and strong performance for each criterion before interviews begin, then compare recorded evidence.

Use the same exercises and the same rubric for every candidate in the role, keep both unchanged for the length of the search, record the evidence behind each score, and have counsel review the loop, since a scored selection process is a selection procedure.

Finish with reference checks run with the candidate's consent. For an individual contributor, ask the former manager whether they would put this person back on their team today and why, how the candidate responded to coaching, and where the candidate ranked against peers. For process fit, verify cycle length, deal size, buyer level, and who made the final decision. Some employers limit what a reference can confirm, so record a policy-limited answer as unavailable instead of treating it as evidence against the candidate. The full consent sequence and question protocol are in reference checks for sales hires.

Frequently asked

How do you assess a salesperson before hiring?

Measure four traits that predict performance: will to sell, coachability, qualification discipline, and process fit. Combine an objective, sales-specific assessment with a real work sample such as a mock discovery call, score every interviewer against a written rubric, and confirm with pointed reference checks. Interviews alone are the least reliable signal.

Why are interviews unreliable for sales roles?

Selling and interviewing use the same skills, so a candidate can win the interview and miss quota. Interviews reward polish and confidence, which sellers are trained to perform, and they encourage pattern matching rather than measurement. You leave feeling certain about a signal that does not predict the outcome.

What is a sales assessment and which one should I use?

A sales assessment is a validated instrument that measures selling ability and sales DNA directly, separate from how the candidate presents. Revenue Bench assesses candidates for selling and sales-leadership roles with Objective Management Group, a sales-specific instrument with predictive validity in the 95 percent range, OMG's published accuracy metric, because it is built to predict selling behavior rather than general personality.

What work sample should I use to test a salesperson?

A mock discovery call is the most revealing, since discovery is where most deals are won or lost. A real-deal role-play using an actual opportunity from your pipeline is a strong second, and for senior roles, a short territory or deal plan tests judgment. A live selling exercise provides evidence that a behavioral answer cannot.

Should I hire for skill or for drive?

Hire for drive, train the skill. OMG data shows tactical skills respond well to training, with Closing rising 55 percent and Comfort Discussing Money 66 percent after training, while Will to Sell moves between 0 and 6 percent across the same training. You can teach someone to close and qualify, but you cannot install the desire to do the hard parts of the job. Screen for sales DNA and drive before the offer, then develop the tactical skills after.

Are DISC or personality tests good for hiring salespeople?

Behavioral-style tests such as DISC describe general working style. They were built for that purpose and later adapted for hiring, and they are not designed to predict on-the-job sales performance. A sales-specific assessment is built to predict selling behavior, and OMG publishes predictive validity in the 95 percent range for its instrument, its own accuracy metric.
