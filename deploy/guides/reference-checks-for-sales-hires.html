Reference check questions for sales candidates: what to ask

By Carlos Garrido, Co-Founder, Revenue BenchPublished August 2, 202614 min read

A reference call usually returns little because it happens late, draws from a list chosen for praise, and relies on questions a former manager can answer with a formula. A consistent protocol, run with the candidate's consent and realistic expectations about employer policy, turns final diligence into a useful screen. The questions below test whether prior managers and colleagues support the candidate's account of the work.

Key takeaways

  • Contact every reference with the candidate's permission, from a list the candidate provides or approves.
  • Keep an employed candidate's current manager off the list unless the candidate gives explicit permission.
  • Ask about scope, performance conditions, working behavior, and willingness to hire again instead of asking for general impressions.
  • Expect some employers to confirm only title and dates, and never count a policy-limited response against the candidate.
  • Use reference evidence to support or challenge past-tense claims, while leaving future-role fit to the other selection screens.
  • Record every reference call on the free template, with the same fields before, during, and after each call.

The ten reference check questions for sales candidates

Ask a sales candidate's references ten questions, grouped into verification, performance context, working reality, and two closing questions. Use the same ten for every finalist. Ask each reference only what that person observed. Run every call with the candidate's consent, from a list the candidate provides or approves, and keep an employed candidate's current manager off it by default.

  1. How did [name]'s responsibilities compare with the rest of the team?
  2. Where did [name] rank on the team while you worked together?
  3. What business did [name] inherit, and what pipeline or customer base did [name] build?
  4. What kinds of deals did [name] handle, including the buyers, deal-size range, and sales-cycle pattern?
  5. How much of [name]'s result came from the territory, product, market timing, or team, and what did [name] contribute personally?
  6. Tell me about a time you gave [name] difficult feedback. What happened next?
  7. How did [name] win deals when performing at their best?
  8. What did you have to manage most closely with [name]?
  9. Would you hire [name] again for this role?
  10. Is there anything I should have asked that I did not?

The question protocol gives what each question surfaces and what a weak answer sounds like. Record a policy-limited answer as unavailable. It is not evidence against the candidate. Follow the consent sequence before contacting any reference, using the list the candidate provides or approves.

Reference check questions for a sales manager or VP of Sales

For a sales manager or VP of Sales hire, keep the ten questions and add four leader questions. Each one names the reference who can answer it from direct observation. A former direct report is the reference who saw how the candidate coached and what standards the candidate set for the team.

  1. Former manager: Would you hire [name] to lead a team again, and why?
  2. Former direct report: Did [name] make you better, and would you work for [name] again?
  3. Former direct report: How did [name] coach the team, and what standards did [name] hold it to?
  4. Former manager: What team and pipeline did [name] inherit, and what did [name] build?

The consent rules apply unchanged to a leadership search. Keep an employed candidate's current employer off the list by default, because contact without explicit permission can cost the candidate their job. A reference call that reaches a contact who knows the incumbent can also expose a confidential VP of Sales replacement, so run leader references at final diligence on the approved list.

Why reference checks return so little

A reference list usually contains people who like the candidate and expect the call. That does not make the list useless. It means a hiring team should choose references by what each person observed, then ask questions that require work evidence instead of praise.

For a seller, start with a former manager who saw the period behind the candidate's claims and add a peer who observed deals or account work. For a sales leader, a former direct report can describe coaching, standards, and operating behavior. The candidate should provide or approve every name.

Employer policy sets another limit. Many companies allow former managers to confirm title and dates while withholding performance data. Quota and attainment specifics often fall inside that restriction. Verify the candidate's account during the structured interview and through attainment evidence the candidate volunteers, such as rankings, awards, or plan letters that describe scope rather than pay, since several US states and cities restrict requesting a candidate's prior compensation records. The account executive interview scorecard shows how to examine track-record claims before references begin.

Softball questions create the rest of the problem. Asking whether someone was a good employee invites adjectives. Asking what business the person inherited, how the person won, what needed management attention, and whether the reference would hire the person again calls for evidence.

Research circulated through OMG's partner network puts hiring-manager intuition at identifying a top performer about 20% of the time. That weak base for intuition is a reason to run final diligence with care instead of treating a positive interview impression as sufficient. The cost of a bad sales hire also includes lost selling time, management attention, and delayed revenue, which raises the value of a disciplined final screen.

Run it with consent, or not at all

Consent governs the process. Ask for references at an agreed stage, explain which calls you want to make, and give the candidate control over any contact that could expose a confidential search.

  1. Ask the finalist for a reference list at the agreed stage, accept names the candidate provides or approves, and get explicit permission for each person before making contact.
  2. Keep the current manager off the list by default for an employed candidate because contacting the current employer without explicit permission can cost the candidate their job.
  3. Tell the candidate whom you plan to call and when, then stay within the approved list and timing.
  4. Expect some employers to confirm only title and dates, and plan the rest of the diligence around that policy limit.
  5. Record what each reference said in the same format and apply the same process to every finalist for the role.

The current-employer rule applies even when a hiring manager knows someone at the candidate's company. The guide to a confidential VP of Sales replacement lists reference calls among the eight routes that expose a contained search, which is why disclosure control matters when a search could affect someone's employment.

Off-list and backchannel calls create consent, confidentiality, and fairness risks. Revenue Bench does not recommend them. If a hiring team believes another source matters, it should name the person to the candidate, explain why, and obtain permission before making contact.

The calls themselves belong to the hiring manager, run live rather than by email, peer to peer with the reference. A useful mix for a sales hire is two former managers who cover the period behind the candidate's claims and one peer or former direct report. Before dialing, confirm the reference through an independent channel, such as the company switchboard or a directory listing that matches the claimed tenure, rather than relying only on a number the candidate supplied.

A company may also need a release or another consent record under its own policy. Confirm that requirement before calling, and tell the reference how the notes will be used and stored.

Selection artifact

The question protocol

Use the former manager as the primary source for rank, feedback, and management questions. A peer or former direct report should answer only what that person observed. Record an answer as unavailable when company policy or the reference's role prevents a response, and do not convert that gap into negative evidence about the candidate.

The question What it surfaces What a weak answer sounds like
Verification
How did [name]'s responsibilities compare with the rest of the team? Whether the candidate carried an individual number, owned a territory, led others, or supported work that someone else owned. The answer repeats a title while hedging on whether the person carried a number or held the scope described.
Where did [name] rank on the team while you worked together? The candidate's relative standing when the reference observed the team and is permitted to discuss it. The answer offers general praise without any comparison, reason, or observed performance context. A policy restriction should be recorded as unavailable instead.
Performance context
What business did [name] inherit, and what pipeline or customer base did [name] build? How much of the result began with an existing book, open opportunities, house accounts, or pipeline the candidate created. The answer credits the result without distinguishing inherited business from work the candidate originated.
What kinds of deals did [name] handle, including the buyers, deal-size range, and sales-cycle pattern? Whether the reference's description of the sales motion supports the role history the candidate gave during interviews. The answer cannot connect the candidate to a buyer type, deal pattern, or part of the sales process.
How much of [name]'s result came from the territory, product, market timing, or team, and what did [name] contribute personally? The conditions behind the result and the candidate's own contribution within them. The answer attributes everything to either the candidate or the environment without describing the work that linked the two.
Working reality
Tell me about a time you gave [name] difficult feedback. What happened next? Whether the candidate understood feedback, changed behavior, and sustained the change after the conversation. The reference cannot name feedback, or the example ends with agreement but no change in later behavior.
How did [name] win deals when performing at their best? The repeatable behaviors behind wins, such as prospecting, discovery, deal control, internal coordination, or executive access. The answer relies on personality labels or relationships without describing an action the candidate repeated.
What did you have to manage most closely with [name]? The operating burden the manager carried, including pipeline discipline, forecasting, preparation, prospecting, follow-through, or team behavior. The answer describes a burden without connecting it to the work, or the reference declines the subject without saying whether policy prevents the answer.
The closing questions
Would you hire [name] again for this role? Whether the reference will make a direct commitment when the target role and its demands are clear. The answer neither commits nor declines, such as, “All I can say is the dates check out.” Where the reference cites company policy, record the response as unavailable rather than weak.
Is there anything I should have asked that I did not? Material context that did not fit the earlier questions, including conditions for success or concerns the reference believes matter. The answer closes immediately after earlier responses stayed general, or repeats praise without adding work evidence.

These questions are Revenue Bench's own protocol, built from the founders' operating experience. Ask the same core set for every finalist, while allowing each reference to answer only from direct observation.

Free reference check template for a sales representative

Revenue Bench's free reference check template for a sales representative is a printable record of one reference call. Before the call, it captures the reference and relationship, the period observed, the candidate's consent, and how identity was confirmed. During the call, it holds the ten answers in the reference's words, with any answer marked unavailable under employer policy. After the call, it logs the hire-again answer, the claims the call supports, and any conflict, with the one follow-up question it raises for the candidate. Use one sheet per reference and the same sheet for every finalist.

After the three reference calls

Lay the three sheets side by side against the candidate's interview account. Mark a claim supported where a reference describes an event the way the candidate did in interview, and note where two references describe the same event. A conflict produces one specific follow-up question for the candidate before the offer. Policy-limited silence is no evidence at all. Then weigh the references with the assessment and the structured interview by what each screen was built to decide. A strong reference does not offset weak selling evidence, as what a reference check cannot decide sets out.

Reading the answers

Useful answers contain events, responsibilities, choices, and consequences. They distinguish what the candidate owned from what the territory, team, or manager supplied. They also stay consistent with the candidate's interview account without sounding copied from it.

The hire-again question compresses the reference's judgment into a commitment. An unqualified yes followed by a role-specific reason carries stronger support than a policy formula or praise that avoids the decision. When the answer is conditional, record the condition because it may identify the environment in which the candidate performed well or struggled.

Judge the content of the answer rather than the reference's manner of speech. Accent, vocabulary, fluency, conversational style, and confidence are not candidate evidence. Focus on what the words establish, which examples support them, what the reference directly observed, and which questions company policy prevented the person from answering.

A reference procedure should stay job-related. Do not ask about family, health, age, origin, disability, religion, or any other protected characteristic. The same boundary covers attendance and leave history, complaints or claims, and criminal history. When a reference volunteers protected information unprompted, redirect the conversation to job-related conduct, leave it out of the notes, and give it no weight in the decision. Apply the same core protocol to every finalist for the role, record evidence consistently, and ask employment counsel to review the procedure before adopting it as standing policy.

What a reference check cannot decide

A reference can describe past work in a known setting. That evidence cannot predict future performance in a different role, company, manager relationship, market, or sales motion. A strong reference may support the candidate's account while leaving the hiring decision open.

References also cannot measure drive and Sales DNA, the underlying selling beliefs and tendencies that influence performance under pressure. Those questions belong to a sales-specific assessment, with the Will to Sell guide covering the drive component and its role in selection.

Quota and attainment confirmation has a similar boundary. A former employer may withhold the information, and the candidate does not control that policy. The hiring team should test the candidate's figures during interviews and review attainment evidence the candidate volunteers, such as rankings, awards, or plan letters that describe scope rather than pay, without judging the candidate for a third party's refusal to confirm them.

References can also conflict with the other screens. A negative reference against strong assessment and interview evidence is a prompt for one specific follow-up question to the candidate rather than a veto, and a strong reference does not offset weak selling evidence. Weigh what each screen was built to decide, and treat policy-limited silence as no evidence at all.

In the full selection sequence, reference checks answer “Does prior performance support the candidate's claims?”, catch third-party confirmation that no other screen provides, cannot see future performance in a different role and company, and run at final diligence before the offer, as shown in the sales assessment versus interview routing table.

Where a hiring team runs reference checks in a Revenue Bench search, they belong at final diligence, after the assessment and the client's own interviews. The hiring team keeps ownership of consent, the calls, the notes, and the decision.

What reference check questions should you ask for a sales manager or VP of Sales?

Should you contact a candidate's current employer for a reference?

The current employer stays off the reference list by default when the candidate is employed. Contact can expose a confidential search and cost the candidate their job, so call only when the candidate gives explicit permission for that person and timing.

Can a reference confirm a salesperson's quota attainment?

Many employers will not confirm quota or attainment because their policy limits responses to title and dates. Verify the candidate's account during the interview and through attainment evidence the candidate volunteers, such as rankings or plan letters that describe scope rather than pay. Several US states and cities restrict requesting prior compensation records, and a third party's refusal to confirm figures is not negative candidate evidence.

When should reference checks happen in the hiring process?

Reference checks belong at final diligence before the offer, after the assessment, structured interview, and any simulation. At that stage, the hiring team has specific claims and role requirements for a former manager or colleague to address.

What if a former employer will only confirm title and dates?

Record the policy-limited response as unavailable information and continue with the evidence the hiring team can control. Do not lower the candidate's standing because an employer refuses to discuss performance, and rely on interview evidence plus any attainment evidence the candidate volunteers.

What does a good answer to “would you hire this person again” sound like?

A useful answer makes a direct commitment and explains why the candidate fits the role being discussed. Conditions can also help when the reference describes the manager, sales motion, support, or expectations the candidate needs to perform well.
