Sales recruiter fees: contingency, retained, and what you pay for

By Carlos Garrido, Co-Founder14 min read

A recruiter's fee structure states who carries the risk if the search goes wrong. The three common models, contingency, container, and retained, move that risk to different places, and the lowest headline percentage is often the most expensive once a mis-hire is priced in. This guide compares the models by the risk each one shifts, then shows the math that decides the real cost of a search.

Key takeaways

  • Three fee models dominate sales recruiting: contingency, paid only if a hire starts; retained, paid in stages whether or not a hire is made; and container or engaged search, with a fixed engagement fee up front and the balance on placement. The difference that matters is who carries the search risk.
  • Contingency looks free until it works, because the recruiter carries all the search cost and only earns on a placement. That incentive rewards speed and volume, and it is why the same role often goes to several firms at once.
  • The fee is the percentage multiplied by the calculation base the agreement names: base salary, on-target earnings, or estimated first-year cash. On a commissioned sales role the choice of base can double the fee at the same percentage, and a flat fee removes the link between the fee and the offer.
  • A small company's recruiter fee follows the same structure as any employer's. For a commissioned first seller, the calculation base moves the fee more than the rate does, and with one to three hired sellers a wrong hire is a third or more of the sales team, so the replacement guarantee matters more than the rate.
  • Retained and container search move some cost to the employer up front, in exchange for exclusivity, depth, and a recruiter whose incentive is the right hire rather than the fast one. It is the standard for senior, confidential, and hard-to-fill roles.
  • The headline percentage is the wrong number to shop. DePaul University research puts the fully loaded cost of sales turnover near $115,000, and Revenue Bench's own working figure puts a revenue-role mis-hire at $200,000 to $250,000 once stalled pipeline and management time are counted. A lower fee on a wrong hire is the most expensive option on offer.
  • A replacement guarantee and an objective assessment change the economics. The guarantee moves post-hire risk back to the recruiter, and assessment-led selection lowers the odds of a wrong hire in the first place.

The three fee models, in plain terms

Contingency search carries no fee until a candidate is hired and starts. The fee is a percentage of a compensation figure the agreement names, and the percentage is set firm by firm and role by role. That compensation figure moves with the market rate for the role. The recruiter funds the whole search and is paid only on success, so the same role is often worked by several firms in parallel.

Retained search is an exclusive search billed in stages regardless of the outcome, commonly in thirds. An engagement fee is due at kickoff, a second payment at shortlist, and the balance at placement. Firms typically use this model for senior, confidential, and scarce roles where depth and discretion matter more than speed.

Container or engaged search is a middle path. A fixed engagement fee up front commits both sides, and the balance is due on placement. It buys exclusivity and priority without a full retainer, and it has grown more common for VP and director roles. Some agreements describe it as a retainer plus a placement fee.

None of these models is better in the abstract. Each prices a different level of commitment and risk. The useful signals are the trigger that determines when the fee is earned and what stands behind the fee after the hire starts.

What sets the cost of a sales search per placement

Under a percentage fee, a sales recruiting firm's cost per placement is the percentage in the agreement multiplied by the compensation figure the agreement names as the calculation base. Revenue Bench publishes no market rate here, because the rate on a real search depends on the variables below, and the only figure that prices your search is the one in the fee clause a firm puts in front of you.

  • The fee model. Contingency is paid on a start. Retained is billed in stages whatever the outcome, and executive-search firms add engagement expenses on top of the fee. Container search adds a fixed engagement fee up front.
  • The calculation base. Base salary, on-target earnings, or estimated first-year cash. On a commissioned role this choice moves the fee more than the percentage does.
  • Seniority and scarcity. Senior roles and scarce profiles take longer to search and more often run retained or engaged, which changes both the model and the terms.
  • Exclusivity and scope. An exclusive search, a multi-hire agreement, or a role that needs assessment and onboarding built in is quoted differently from a single non-exclusive search.
  • What stands behind the fee. The replacement guarantee, its window, and what voids it decide how much of the fee survives a hire that fails early.

For a salaried role with no commission, base salary, on-target earnings, and first-year cash are close to the same figure, so the calculation base changes little. For a commissioned seller it matters, because on-target earnings add the commission target to base salary. Read the calculation base before comparing percentages across firms: a lower percentage on a larger base can cost more.

Flat-fee recruiting for a sales team

A flat-fee search charges a fixed price agreed before the search opens, so the cost of each hire is known in advance and does not move with the commission plan or the final offer. A company hiring several sellers on the same pay plan can budget the search cost per seat. Nobody has to agree on how much commission a new seller will earn before the invoice can be written. The recruiter also gains nothing from a higher offer, so the fee gives the recruiter no stake in the salary negotiation.

A flat fee fixes the amount of the fee. The fixed price can still be paid up front, in stages, or only when a hire starts, so the questions in the comparison above apply to it as well: which event earns the fee, who funds the search if no hire is made, and whether a replacement guarantee stands behind the result. On a hard search that runs longer than planned, ask how the firm staffs the search once the fixed price is used up.

Revenue Bench prices its searches as a percentage of the hire's first-year compensation, so a higher offer raises its fee. Setting out what a flat fee does well runs against that interest.

What a sales recruiter costs a small business

A small business pays a sales recruiter the fee its agreement sets: a percentage multiplied by the calculation base the agreement names, or a flat amount fixed before the search. Early sellers usually earn commission, so the calculation base moves the fee more than the rate does, as the fee-basis table shows. With one to three hired sellers, a wrong hire is a third or more of the sales team, so the replacement guarantee behind the fee matters more than the rate. A company that hires a seller or two a year spreads any internal recruiting function across few hires, so each hire carries more of that function's cost.

Revenue Bench publishes no market rate for a small-company search because terms vary by search, as the cost-per-placement section explains. The figure that prices the search is the one in the fee clause, computed on your company's own offer.

What the fee is really pricing

Shopping the headline percentage optimizes the smallest number in the decision. A recruiter's fee is a fraction of what the search puts at risk, because the larger cost arrives when the hire is wrong.

DePaul University's Center for Sales Leadership, in its 2011-2012 Sales Effectiveness Survey, surveyed 435 B2B sales organizations and put the average cost of sales turnover near $49,508. That figure rises to roughly $115,000 when fully loaded.

Revenue Bench uses a working figure of $200,000 to $250,000 for a revenue-role mis-hire. That estimate counts base compensation, draw, stalled pipeline, the replacement search, and management time.

On a senior search, the gap between a lower fee and a higher one is small next to the gap between a right hire and a wrong one, which runs to six figures and a year of a dead territory. A fee structure that lowers the odds of the wrong hire is cheaper at any headline rate.

The alternative of running the search internally carries its own cost. Our guide to choosing a sales recruiter versus an in-house search compares where each route's cost sits and who carries it when a hire fails early.

Where a guarantee changes the math

Two levers move the economics of a recruiter fee.

  1. A replacement guarantee moves post-hire risk back to the recruiter. If the hire does not last the covered window, the firm runs the search again at no new fee. That turns a one-shot fee into a fee that stands behind the result. Terms differ widely, and length alone does not decide the value. What matters is what the placement guarantee covers and what voids it. The same guide answers which search firms offer a 90-day placement guarantee.
  2. An objective, sales-specific assessment puts evidence on the candidate before the guarantee is ever tested. In Objective Management Group's 2024 validation survey, 72% of candidates OMG recommended reached the top half of their sales force within 12 months. First-year turnover ran 9% for recommended hires against 33% for candidates it advised against. These are OMG's own figures, from the group that has assessed sales talent since 1990. Our guide to assessing sales candidates before hiring explains how that evidence fits into selection.

A fee attached to an assessment and a guarantee is priced against a lower risk than a bare contingency fee on an unscreened candidate, even when the headline percentage is higher.

How to read a fee structure before you sign

  1. Find one trigger, clearly stated. Ask when the fee is earned and whether it is tied to the hire's start date. A precise trigger is the clean answer. Language that leaves several possible billing events is the warning sign.
  2. Ask what stands behind it. Confirm whether there is a replacement guarantee and whether every condition is in writing. A defined remedy, window, and set of conditions read cleanly. Verbal assurances leave the employer carrying the risk.
  3. Read what the length of the agreement changes. Where a firm proposes an agreement running across several searches or several years, the guarantee and the reporting become standing terms. The checks to settle first are set out in what to fix in writing before a multi-year agreement.
  4. Look for one number rather than layers. A single percentage or flat fee makes comparison possible. Stacked fees and vague triggers make the true cost impossible to compare across firms.
  5. Establish what survives a failed hire. Ask whether any fee stays earned if the hire does not last and on what terms. The answer should state the obligation and remedy without interpretation.

Frequently asked

What is the difference between contingency and retained search?

Contingency means you pay only if a candidate is hired and starts, commonly a percentage of first-year compensation; the recruiter carries the full search cost and the role is often open to several firms at once. Retained search is exclusive and billed in stages regardless of outcome, commonly in thirds across kickoff, shortlist, and placement; it is used for senior, confidential, and hard-to-fill roles where depth and discretion matter more than speed.

What is a typical sales recruiter fee?

A recruiter fee follows a model more than a single market number. Contingency and direct-hire firms charge a percentage of the hire's compensation, due when the hire starts. Retained executive search charges a fee tied to estimated first-year compensation plus engagement expenses, billed in stages, the structure described in Korn Ferry's and Heidrick and Struggles' annual reports. Container search adds a fixed engagement fee up front. On a given search the fee moves with the calculation base, the seniority and scarcity of the role, exclusivity, and the guarantee behind it, so ask each firm for its fee clause in writing and compare the earning trigger, the calculation base, and the replacement terms. The headline percentage is a small fraction of what a wrong hire costs.

Which fee structure is best?

None is best in the abstract. Contingency fits high-volume or individual-contributor roles where the market is deep and speed matters. Retained or engaged search fits senior, confidential, and scarce roles where a wrong hire is expensive and discretion is required. The right choice is the one whose incentive matches the risk in your specific search.

Why do retained searches cost money up front?

Because the fee pays for exclusivity and depth. A retained recruiter's cost is covered whether or not a placement is made, so the incentive shifts to finding the right hire rather than the fastest one. For a senior or confidential role, that alignment is usually worth more than the cash saved by a contingency arrangement.

Does a placement guarantee reduce the risk of the fee?

Yes, when the terms are real. A replacement guarantee means the recruiter runs the search again at no new fee if the hire does not last the window, which moves post-hire risk back to the firm. Revenue Bench places a replacement guarantee behind every hire and assesses candidates for selling and sales-leadership roles with Objective Management Group before presentation, so the fee is priced against a lower risk of a wrong hire.
