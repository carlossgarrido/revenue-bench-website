Should You Promote Your Best Rep Into Sales Management?

By Steve Swanston, Co-Founder · Founder, Swanston Growth Advisors, a Certified Partner of Objective Management Group9 min read

Promoting your best salesperson into sales management is the default move, and the data runs against the default. In Objective Management Group's evaluation of 44,493 sales managers, only 9% were strong in all three coaching qualities. Managers strong in all three have 80% more elite salespeople on their teams, a comparison of manager groups that does not show the qualities as the cause. Those are OMG's own figures, and they point to a fact most promotions skip past: selling ability and coaching ability are different things, each measurable on its own, and being excellent at the first tells you almost nothing about the second. The best rep is sometimes the right choice for the seat. The odds do not favor assuming it.

Why the best rep gets the job

When a management seat opens, the top performer is the obvious name on the whiteboard. The logic feels sound. This person already outsells everyone, knows the product and the buyer cold, and has the team's respect. Promoting them rewards the results, keeps them from leaving, and signals to everyone else that production is what gets you ahead. Every incentive in the moment points the same direction.

The flattery works on both sides. The company gets to reward its best without running a real search, and the rep hears the promotion as the natural next rung on the ladder. Turning it down can feel like refusing a raise and a vote of confidence at once. So the offer gets made and accepted on momentum, and nobody puts the question of whether this person can develop other people to the rep.

The operator reality arrives a quarter or two later. The new manager is still closing their own deals, because that is what they are good at. The team is under-coached, because coaching was never the strength being rewarded. The person who used to carry one number now carries eight of them, with tools they were never assessed for. The promotion took care of the retention worry and left a management problem in its place, one nobody had measured for and nobody had written down in a 30-60-90 scorecard the new manager could work to.

What the manager data shows

Set the flattery aside and look at the manager data. OMG has assessed 44,493 sales managers, and the finding that matters for this decision is scarcity: only 9% are strong in all three coaching qualities. Nine in a hundred. Whoever you promote is far more likely to land outside that group than inside it, and no amount of personal selling talent moves them into it, because the three qualities are not selling qualities.

In OMG's data, managers strong in all three coaching qualities have 80% more elite salespeople on their teams than managers who are not. Strength in all three is rare and can be measured, so it is worth assessing before the promotion decision.

OMG's coaching-cadence study adds a second measure. OMG studied 11,078 salespeople and their managers and found that reps coached several times a week scored 17 percentile points higher than reps never coached, a comparison of groups coached at different cadences that does not show coaching as the cause. The practice is rare: only about 10% of reps get coached several times a week. Revenue Bench recommends assessing a prospective manager's willingness and ability to coach on a regular cadence.

Selling and coaching are different skills

The three qualities OMG measures in a sales manager all concern developing other people. Reduced to plain terms, they are the ability to coach, meaning to watch someone sell and change how they do it through specific feedback; the ability to motivate, meaning to understand what drives each different person on the team rather than assuming everyone runs on what drives you; and the discipline to develop a team through a steady cadence rather than sporadic heroics. None of the three shows up on a commission statement, which is why a top seller can be strong on every one of them or none, and the sales number will not tell you which.

A great closer usually wins on personal strengths: instincts sharpened over years that live in one person and cannot be handed over. They read a buyer in seconds, improvise the right question, sense when to push and when to wait. Those instincts made them elite, and they resist transfer, because the closer often cannot say what they did, only that it worked. Ask a natural closer to teach a struggling rep and the common answer is some version of do what I do, which describes a talent the other person does not have rather than a method they can follow. That gap between doing and teaching is the same wrong-role problem we traced across the hiring funnel in why sales hires fail, moved up one level to the manager's chair.

There is a second trap. The self-reliance and competitive drive that make someone a strong individual seller can work against them in management, where the job is to make other people win and to take the reward at one remove. A manager who keeps stepping in to close the team's deals is feeding the seller's instinct and starving the coaching the team needs. The very drive that built their track record starts working against the people they now manage.

The cost of getting it wrong

A weak individual seller costs you a single quota. In Revenue Bench's judgment, a weak manager's cost reaches every seller who reports to them and lasts as long as they hold the role. The manager decision deserves more scrutiny than any single rep hire.

In OMG's 2024 validation survey, first-year turnover ran 9% for hires the assessment recommended and 33% for candidates it advised against who were hired anyway. That is a selection figure, and it does not measure management. Revenue Bench's view is that a weak hire under a manager who cannot coach a slow start has no correction left.

Each of those exits carries a price. DePaul University's Center for Sales Leadership, surveying 435 B2B sales organizations, puts the average cost of a sales departure near $49,508, and close to $115,000 fully loaded once you count lost production, the replacement search, and management time. Run a team of eight that loses a third of its sellers in a year, and the arithmetic reaches six figures before the manager's own salary enters the calculation. The promotion also opens the rep's former territory, an exposure you can price with the vacancy ledger.

How to decide

None of this argues against ever promoting a top seller. Some of them become excellent managers. It argues for deciding on evidence, and four moves do that.

  1. Assess for the management job. Run a sales-management assessment on the candidate the way you would on an outside applicant for the same seat, the same discipline we lay out in how to assess a salesperson, pointed at the management role. You are measuring the ability to coach, motivate, and develop a team, none of which the sales quota reports. The same three qualities decide the case of a sales leader you inherited rather than chose, where the evidence has to be separated from the numbers the previous owner produced. Treat the promotion as filling a distinct role with its own bar, interviewed against the same sales leader interview scorecard an outside candidate would face.
  2. Look for evidence they already develop people. The clearest signal is behavior the person shows before any title changes. Do they help newer reps without being asked, run a useful deal review, explain their own method in terms someone else can put to work? A rep who has spent a year coaching teammates on their own initiative is a different prospect from one whose entire record is personal production.
  3. Keep the dual track open. The assumption that advancement means management costs companies their best sellers every year. When the person's talent and preference is selling, build a senior individual-contributor track that pays for elite production, so a rainmaker can keep making rain at senior-IC economics while you fill the management seat with a genuine coach. Bringing in that coach from outside looks more like hiring a VP of sales than like rewarding a rep, and it leaves you with a real developer plus the revenue the rainmaker was always going to produce.
  4. If you do promote, coach the new manager through the transition. A promoted seller is a new hire in a role they have never held, so run their first 90 days with the structure you would give any new hire. Meet the new manager weekly, work on the three qualities the seat demands, and hold the cadence past the point where early wins tempt everyone to ease off. OMG's coaching figures describe reps, and no study cited here measures coaching for new managers, so the weekly meeting is Revenue Bench's own guidance.

This is how we handle it when a client is weighing the promotion, and it is part of how we work with employers: we assess the candidate against the management role, put the dual-track option in front of the client, and, when the promotion is right, build the onboarding that gives the new manager a real chance at the 9% group rather than the 91%.

Methodology and sources

How to read these numbers

The manager figures come from Objective Management Group's evaluation of 44,493 sales managers: the 9% who were strong in all three coaching qualities, and the finding that managers strong in all three have 80% more elite salespeople on their teams, a comparison that does not establish cause. The coaching-cadence figures come from OMG's study of 11,078 salespeople and their managers: reps coached several times a week scored 17 percentile points higher than reps never coached, a group comparison that does not establish cause, and roughly 10% of reps are coached several times a week. OMG had assessed nearly 2.4 million salespeople as of 2023, which OMG describes as the largest sales-specific dataset in existence.

Figures attributed to OMG are OMG's own published data, analyzed here by a Certified Partner firm rather than independent peer-reviewed research, and we label them that way. The first-year turnover figures, 9% against 33%, come from OMG's 2024 validation survey. Cost figures are third-party: DePaul University's Center for Sales Leadership puts the average cost of a sales departure near $49,508 and close to $115,000 fully loaded.

Frequently asked

Should top salespeople become sales managers?

Sometimes, and the base rate cautions against assuming it. In OMG's evaluation of 44,493 sales managers, only 9% were strong in all three coaching qualities, and those qualities are separate from the ones that make someone a great closer. Selling talent does not carry into coaching talent on its own. Promote a top rep only after assessing them for the management role, and keep a senior individual-contributor track so strong sellers can advance without managing anyone.

How do you assess someone for a sales management role?

Measure the job you are filling rather than the one they already do. A sales-management assessment reads the ability to coach, motivate, and develop a team, which a sales quota does not report. Alongside it, look for evidence the person already develops others: helping newer reps on their own initiative, running useful deal reviews, and explaining their method so someone else can follow it. In OMG's data only 9% of managers are strong in all three coaching qualities, so the assessment is what separates the exception from the average.

Why do great salespeople often struggle as managers?

Because selling and coaching are different skills. A great closer wins on personal instincts that are hard to explain and harder to transfer, and the self-reliance that made them elite can pull them toward closing the team's deals instead of developing the team. OMG's study of 44,493 managers found only 9% strong in all three coaching qualities, and personal selling ability does not move someone into that group.

What does it cost to promote the wrong person into sales management?

In Revenue Bench's judgment, a weak manager's cost reaches every seller who reports to them. What is the alternative to promoting your best rep?

A dual-track career path.
