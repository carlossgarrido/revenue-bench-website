The 6% problem: what the data says about sales talent

By Carlos Garrido, Co-Founder, Revenue Bench10 min read

In OMG's data, 6% of salespeople are elite, 11% strong, 33% serviceable, and half weak. Effort is rarely the constraint in OMG's data. In Revenue Bench's view, OMG's tier distribution is a reason to test candidates before trusting an instinct read. Those odds matter because the traits that predict selling are rare, invisible in an interview, and easy to confuse with the ones that do not matter. A sales-specific assessment beats the interview at reading those odds, which is what the rest of this guide is about.

Key takeaways

  • In OMG's data, the talent curve is steep: 6% elite, 11% strong, 33% serviceable, 50% weak. Half of OMG's assessed population is weak, so an unmeasured hire starts against that base rate.

  • Interviews cannot see any of this. Hiring-manager intuition identifies a future top performer only about 20% of the time, OMG-cited.
  • What predicts performance is measurable, and in OMG's 2024 validation survey 72% of recommended hires reach the top half of the team within a year.
  • Screen with a sales-specific assessment before the first interview and hire for Will to Sell because training barely moves it in OMG's study. Plan coaching for Sales DNA and tactical skills, which changed after training in that study.

The distribution: 6, 11, 33, 50

Objective Management Group had assessed nearly 2.4 million salespeople as of 2023. Sorted by measured selling ability, the population breaks into four tiers, and the curve is steeper than almost any leader expects.

Read it straight and the implication is uncomfortable. Only about one in six salespeople is strong or elite, and half are weak. If you hire from the open market without a way to tell the tiers apart, the base rate is working against you, and it presses hardest on a founder's first two or three sellers, where there is no larger team to absorb a bad draw. You are far more likely to draw from the bottom half than the top, the weak-pool pattern traced first in why sales hires fail.

This is the 6% problem. The talent you want is scarce, the talent you do not is abundant, and the two are hard to tell apart in the moment you most need to.

Why most teams misread it

Faced with an underperforming team, most leaders reach for the wrong explanation. They assume the problem is effort, so they push harder: more activity, more pressure, a bigger number on the wall. OMG's data says effort is rarely the constraint. About 86% of salespeople have Desire, the wanting, and about 88% handle rejection well, in OMG's data. Most sellers show up and take the hits. Wanting it is almost never what separates the 6% from the 50%.

The gap sits in a place a manager cannot see by watching activity: sales DNA, the set of underlying beliefs and instincts that either support selling or sabotage it from below. Here the numbers invert. In OMG's data, only 12% of salespeople are strong on supportive beliefs, which means 88% carry beliefs that hold them back, often without knowing it. A seller who believes price is the issue, or who needs to be liked, or who is uncomfortable talking about money, will undermine themselves in ways no amount of effort fixes.

So the team is misread twice. The trait that looks like the problem, effort, is usually fine. The trait that is the problem, sales DNA, is invisible to the naked eye. Leaders end up coaching the wrong thing harder, and the curve does not move.

Where the sales talent gap sits

The sales talent gap is the distance between what a selling role needs and what the seller brings. In OMG's data, desire is common at 86% strong, and handling rejection is 88% to 89% strong. The traits that separate sellers are rare: 12% are strong on supportive beliefs (2022 to 2025), 11% to 15% on consultative selling, and 16% are proficient in sales technology (2025).

Why interviews miss the 6%

Most teams try to detect the talent gap in the interview, the step least able to identify it. Hiring-manager intuition identifies a future top performer only about 20% of the time, an OMG-cited figure, which is worse than it sounds, because it comes paired with high confidence.

The reason is structural. Selling and interviewing use the same muscles: rapport, storytelling, reading the other person, and handling objections. A strong interviewer can be a weak closer, and a reserved, methodical closer can interview poorly. The interview rewards polish and presence, which are the things a weak seller with high need for approval performs best. The 6% do not always present as the strongest candidate, and the 50% sometimes do.

It gets worse with pattern matching. Interviewers favor candidates who remind them of sellers they have known, which feels like judgment but comes from familiarity. The result is a process that filters for confidence and likeness, neither of which predicts quota, and a steep talent curve that the interview is structurally unable to climb.

The traits that predict performance

The good news is that selling ability is measurable, and far more precisely than instinct allows. OMG's objective, sales-specific assessment looks at 21 competencies across more than 400 data points per candidate, grouped into three questions: does the person want it, what is holding them back beneath the surface, and can they do the work. We walk through how to run that read in how to assess sales candidates before hiring.

  • Will to Sell. Desire, Commitment, Motivation, Responsibility, and Outlook. The engine, and the one group you cannot install later.
  • Sales DNA. Comfort Discussing Money, Need for Approval, controlling emotions and staying in the moment, supportive beliefs, a supportive buy cycle, and how the person handles rejection. The hidden layer, and where the gap usually lives.
  • Tactical Selling. Hunting, reaching decision makers, relationship building, consultative selling, selling value, qualifying, presentation approach, closing, sales process, and sales technology. The skills of the job itself.

Measured this way, the signal is strong where the interview is weak. OMG's documentation puts the assessment's predictive validity in the 95 percent range, and in OMG's 2024 validation survey, 72% of the candidates it recommends reach the top half of the team within 12 months. The reverse holds too: 100% of candidates it flagged as not recommended, who were hired anyway, landed in the bottom half. First-year turnover in that data tells the same story, 9% for recommended hires against 33% for those not recommended.

OMG's position is that behavioral-style assessments such as DISC are not predictive of on-the-job performance. A sales-specific assessment measures the competencies in the gap table directly.

What this means for hiring

The distribution does not have to work against you. Once you can see the tiers, the hiring moves are clear, and they run opposite to instinct.

  1. Screen with a sales-specific assessment. Put it at the front of the process, before the first interview. It is the only step that reliably separates the 6% and the 11% from the half of the market you do not want, before an interview talks you into the wrong call.
  2. Hire for drive, and weigh sales DNA. Will to Sell moved 0% to 6% after training in OMG's study of 5,331 salespeople, so treat it as a hiring input. Sales DNA also changed after training, with Comfort Discussing Money up 66%, and OMG describes that change as slow and sequential: the belief shifts, then the behavior, then the skill holds. Weigh a deep gap in supportive beliefs before the offer.
  3. Train the skill after. In OMG's study of 5,331 salespeople, Closing rose 55%, Reaching decision makers rose 43%, and Consultative selling rose 28% after training. Plan that coaching into the first 90 days. The full case for treating drive as a hiring input, marker by marker, is in the Will to Sell guide.

In OMG's evaluation of 44,493 sales managers, managers strong in all three coaching qualities have 80% more elite salespeople on their teams, and 9% of managers are strong in all three. Who leads the team belongs in the hiring plan with who sells, including any decision about promoting your best rep into management.

The 6% problem is a measurement problem. The talent is rare, but it is not hidden once you stop relying on the one tool that cannot find it. Methodology and sources

How to read these numbers

Every figure in this guide but one is Objective Management Group's (OMG) data. The four tiers come from OMG's dataset of nearly 2.4 million salespeople assessed as of 2023, each measured on 21 sales-specific competencies across more than 400 data points. The training, sales-manager and outcome figures come from separate OMG studies named where they appear. The exception is the 20% hiring-intuition figure, research by the firm Big Swift Kick that circulated through OMG's partner network. The four tiers, 6% elite, 11% strong, 33% serviceable, and 50% weak, sort that assessed population by measured selling ability. Each percentage is the share of assessed salespeople in that tier and does not forecast any single team.

This is OMG's own dataset, which OMG describes as the largest sales-specific dataset in existence, and it is not independent, peer-reviewed research; each OMG figure on this page is labeled as OMG's data for that reason. Revenue Bench's searches include the OMG assessment, so Revenue Bench has an interest in readers trusting these figures. The limits on each figure are set out on the sources page. Predictive validity "in the 95 percent range" is OMG's published accuracy metric for its assessment and is distinct from the outcome figures. The outcome numbers, 72% of recommended hires reaching the top half and 9% versus 33% first-year turnover, are from OMG's 2024 validation survey, the current and more conservative of OMG's two outcome eras.

Revenue Bench is a founder-led firm and assesses candidates for selling and sales-leadership roles with OMG through co-founder Steve Swanston's Certified Partner firm, Swanston Growth Advisors. We publish these figures as OMG's evidence, clearly attributed, so a hiring team can weigh them for what they are.
