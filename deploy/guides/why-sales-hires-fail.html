Why sales hires fail: what 2.4 million assessments show

By Steve Swanston, Co-Founder · Founder, Swanston Growth Advisors, a Certified Partner of Objective Management Group9 min read

Before your next sales hire, check which failure patterns your hiring process lets through. Objective Management Group had assessed nearly 2.4 million salespeople as of 2023, which OMG describes as the largest sales-specific dataset in existence. Steve Swanston's reading of OMG's published data identifies five patterns; our ten-question hiring-process audit helps you locate the gaps in your own process.

Key takeaways

  • The five patterns enter at identifiable steps, from sourcing and screening through the first 90 days. Review those steps before opening another search.
  • A bottom-heavy talent pool and traits an interview cannot reveal call for a sales-specific assessment before the first interview.
  • Define the selling motion before sourcing. A candidate's prior success needs to match the demands of the role you are filling.
  • Agree how assessment warnings will affect the final decision, and assign responsibility for coaching and ramp checkpoints before the hire starts.
  • Use the ten-question hiring-process audit to identify exposed patterns and the process steps that need attention.

Why do sales hires fail?

Most failed sales hires trace to five patterns, identified in Steve Swanston's reading of published data from Objective Management Group (OMG): a bottom-heavy talent pool, selling traits an interview cannot reveal, capable people placed in the wrong role, assessment warnings overridden at the offer, and a first 90 days without consistent management. Each pattern enters at a specific step of the hiring process: sourcing and screening, the interview, the role definition, the final decision, or the first 90 days. Checking those five steps before the next search shows where a process lets failures through.

Pattern 1: The pool is bottom-heavy

Sorted by measured selling ability, OMG's database of assessed salespeople breaks into four tiers: 6% elite, 11% strong, 33% serviceable, and 50% weak. A search limited to incoming applicants, with no objective screen for selling ability, leaves the company unable to distinguish those tiers. The 6% problem explains the distribution and its implications for sourcing.

Where it enters the process: sourcing and screening, when the company decides whom the search reaches and what evidence qualifies someone for an interview.

Pattern 2: The traits that sink a hire are invisible in the interview

In OMG's data, desire is strong in 86% of salespeople, and 88% handle rejection well. Only 12% of salespeople are strong on supportive beliefs, meaning 88% carry beliefs that cap their own performance. Across 1,500+ evaluations from 2023 to 2025, only 27% of salespeople showed strong Sales DNA overall, OMG's term for the beliefs and instincts that support or work against selling. Those limiting beliefs include thinking price is why deals die, needing the prospect's approval, and discomfort discussing money, and an interview cannot establish any of them.

A hiring manager's read alone identifies a future top performer about 20% of the time. That figure comes from research by the firm Big Swift Kick, circulated through OMG's partner network; it is not an OMG published study. The miss comes with confidence attached, because interviewing and selling use the same surface skills. Our assessment and interview guide explains which evidence belongs to each screen. Where it enters the process: the interview, when a persuasive conversation becomes the evidence for selling ability.

Pattern 3: Right person, wrong role

OMG's role-fit data puts 57% of salespeople in roles they are not suited for, and 64% of sales teams significantly misaligned. A seller's success in account expansion does not establish their ability to win new enterprise accounts. The selling motion and buyer level need to be defined before the search begins.

In OMG's study of 44,493 sales managers, only 9% had all three of the coaching qualities that develop elite salespeople. The same role-fit question applies when promoting your best rep into sales management. Where it enters the process: the role definition, when the company sets requirements without specifying the work and conditions in which the person must succeed.

Pattern 4: The warning was on the page, and the hire happened anyway

In OMG's 2024 validation survey, 72% of candidates the assessment recommended, who were then hired, reached the top half of their sales force within 12 months. Of the candidates flagged "not recommended" who were hired anyway, 100% landed in the bottom half. First-year turnover ran 9% for recommended hires against 33% for not-recommended hires. These are associations between groups; they do not establish that the assessment caused the differences.

OMG reports predictive validity "in the 95 percent range," a separate metric from those hiring outcomes. The assessment and interview guide explains that distinction and the evidence each screen supplies. Agree on an override rule before a preferred candidate reaches the offer stage. Where it enters the process: the final decision, when the team hires against a warning without an agreed review and approval rule.

Pattern 5: The first 90 days go unmanaged

Industry data from CSO Insights has 40% of companies saying a new rep needs 10 or more months to reach full productivity. In OMG's study of 11,078 salespeople and their managers, coaching several times a week was associated with a gain of 17 percentile points against never coaching; weekly coaching was associated with a gain of 9. Only 10% of salespeople get coached several times a week, 20% weekly, and 8% get no coaching at all.

Meanwhile 80% to 90% of sales training fails to produce lasting change when it is run without a competency diagnosis first, so the fix most companies reach for, a generic training course, does not catch the falling hire either. Our 90-day onboarding plan supplies the ramp checkpoints and calendar tool. Where it enters the process: the first 90 days, when coaching has no named owner and the hire has no dated ramp to follow.

Which patterns your hiring process lets through

Answer for the process your company follows today, using the most recent search as evidence. Each pattern has two questions. Once both are answered, either No marks that pattern as exposed. Your answers stay on this page; nothing is stored or sent anywhere.

Pattern 1: The pool

1. Does every candidate for a selling or sales-leadership role take a sales-specific assessment before the first interview?

2. Does the search reach salespeople who are not applying for jobs this month?

Pattern 2: Invisible traits

3. Is each candidate scored on a written scorecard agreed before the first interview?

4. Does the decision use evidence on traits an interview cannot show, such as need for approval or comfort discussing money?

Pattern 3: Wrong role

5. Is the selling motion written down before sourcing starts: hunting or farming, transactional or enterprise, new logo or expansion?

6. Is each finalist's past success checked against that same motion and buyer level?

Pattern 4: The ignored warning

7. Is there a written rule for what happens when the assessment does not recommend a candidate the team likes?

8. Does someone other than the hiring manager have to approve hiring against a "not recommended" result?

Pattern 5: The unmanaged first 90 days

9. Is one named person responsible for coaching the hire every week for the first 90 days?

10. Is the ramp written down with dated checkpoints, and does the hire see it on day one?

Answer both questions in a pattern to score it.

An exposed pattern identifies a process step to review before your next hire. Follow its link, examine the No answers, and assign someone to close the gap. Multiple patterns can be exposed; the checklist does not rank their severity or predict an individual candidate's outcome. A completed pattern with two Yes answers has no exposure on these questions, but the pattern can still occur.

What the failure costs

DePaul University's Center for Sales Leadership, in its 2011-2012 Sales Effectiveness Survey of 435 B2B sales organizations, puts the average cost of sales turnover near $49,508, rising to roughly $115,000 fully loaded. The same DePaul survey found 33% of all sales turnover happening in the first year of tenure, where the denominator is total turnover. In the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' Employee Tenure release of September 24, 2026, median tenure in sales and related occupations was 3.3 years in January 2026, against 4.1 years across all wage and salary workers. That measure is median tenure and BLS publishes no first-year rate for any occupation.

Our own working figure for a revenue-role mis-hire, counting base, draw, stalled pipeline, the replacement search, and management time, is $200,000 to $250,000. The full breakdown is in The true cost of a bad sales hire.

Hiring against the pattern

The countermeasures below belong in the hiring process.

  1. Screen objectively before the interviews. Put a sales-specific assessment at the front of the process, while the decision remains open and before the team forms a preference.
  2. Respect the recommendation. In OMG's 2024 validation survey, 100% of overridden "not recommended" hires landed in the bottom half. If you pay for the evidence, use it.
  3. Match the person to the role. Define the selling motion first: hunting or farming, transactional or enterprise, new logo or expansion. Then screen for that role specifically, scoring the conversation against the account executive interview scorecard. It is the direct counter to the 57% wrong-role figure.
  4. Manage the first 90 days. Weekly coaching for the hire, weekly alignment with the manager, for 90 days. OMG's coaching study associates weekly coaching with a gain of 9 percentile points against never coaching.

This sequence is the Revenue Bench process: candidates for selling and sales-leadership roles assessed with OMG before presentation, every candidate screened by a sales operator, matched to the specific selling motion, trained before day one, and coached through the first 90 days, with a 90-day replacement guarantee standing behind the hire.

Methodology and sources

How to read these numbers

The dataset behind this analysis belongs to Objective Management Group (OMG), which had assessed nearly 2.4 million salespeople as of 2023, measuring 21 sales-specific competencies across 400+ data points per person. Figures attributed to OMG are OMG's own published data, analyzed here by a certified partner; they are not independent peer-reviewed research, and we label them accordingly.

Outcome figures (72%, 100%, 9% vs 33%) come from OMG's 2024 validation survey, the current and more conservative of the two eras of OMG outcome data; earlier, widely circulated figures are higher. Predictive validity "in the 95 percent range" is OMG's published accuracy metric and is distinct from the outcome figures. Cost and productivity figures are third-party: DePaul University's Center for Sales Leadership, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, and CSO Insights. Each source behind a figure on this site is listed with its producer, sample and period in the provenance index.

The hiring-process audit on this page is Revenue Bench's own checklist; it is separate from OMG research, and no statistic is attached to it. Revenue Bench is paid when a company hires through one of its searches, and several audit questions describe steps its own process includes.

Frequently asked

Why do most new sales hires fail?

Rarely for lack of effort. Across OMG's dataset of nearly 2.4 million assessed salespeople, the recurring causes are a bottom-heavy talent pool (only 6% elite, 50% weak), disqualifying traits an interview cannot detect (88% of salespeople carry self-limiting beliefs), placement into the wrong type of sales role (57% of salespeople), ignored assessment warnings, and an unmanaged first 90 days.

How often do sales hires fail?

In DePaul University's 2011-2012 Sales Effectiveness Survey, 33% of all sales turnover happened in the first year of tenure, where the denominator is total turnover. In the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' Employee Tenure release of September 24, 2026, median tenure in sales and related occupations was 3.3 years in January 2026, against 4.1 years across all wage and salary workers. BLS publishes no first-year rate for any occupation. For candidates hired against an OMG "not recommended" flag, the 2024 validation survey found first-year turnover of 33% and 100% of retained hires landing in the bottom half of the team.

Can you predict whether a sales hire will succeed?

To a measurable degree, yes. OMG's sales-specific assessment carries predictive validity in the 95 percent range. In its 2024 validation survey, 72% of recommended candidates who were hired reached the top half of their sales force within 12 months, and first-year turnover for recommended hires was 9% against 33% for not-recommended hires.

Is effort the reason sales hires fail?

Almost never. In OMG's data, 86% of salespeople have strong desire and 88% handle rejection well. The failure traits live in Sales DNA: only 12% are strong on supportive beliefs and only 27% show strong Sales DNA overall. Those traits are invisible in interviews, which is why effort gets blamed and the real cause goes unmeasured.

What does a failed sales hire cost?

DePaul University's Center for Sales Leadership, in its 2011-2012 Sales Effectiveness Survey of 435 B2B sales organizations, puts average sales turnover cost near $49,508 and roughly $115,000 fully loaded. Revenue Bench's working figure for a revenue-role mis-hire, counting stalled pipeline and management time, is $200,000 to $250,000.
